Senior discovery spans people with different authority, incentives, vocabularies and evidence. In the reconciliation case, a regional operations lead may want local flexibility while group finance wants standard controls and engineering wants fewer integrations. The senior BA creates a process where the disagreement can be understood and decided.

## Design a stakeholder strategy

> **In short:** Prioritize engagement by decision authority, impact, knowledge and risk.

A stakeholder list is not an engagement plan. For each person or group, record what they know, how the change affects them, which decisions they can make, what evidence they trust and how they need to participate. Include people who perform exception work, operate systems, own controls and receive the reports.

Distinguish decision-makers from subject-matter experts and people affected by adoption. A senior sponsor may authorize investment but cannot describe a frontline workaround. A control owner can define evidence requirements but should not speak for all users.

Use a power/impact map to find gaps, then choose the lightest useful engagement: one-to-one interview, working session, prototype review, decision forum or asynchronous feedback. Revisit the map as the initiative changes; influence and impact shift over time.

## Lead executive discovery

> **In short:** Use executive time to clarify outcomes, constraints, decisions and trade-offs.

Executives need a concise conversation tied to choices. Send a short pre-read with the decision or question, known evidence and unresolved assumptions. Ask what result matters most, what cannot be compromised, what options have already been rejected, who owns the decision and by when.

Do not turn the meeting into a status readout or a requirements interview. Use follow-ups to separate a stated solution from the need behind it. “We need one global platform” may mean leaders need consistent control evidence, not identical screens in every region.

Close by summarizing the decision, rationale, conditions, owner and open questions. Confirm the summary afterward. If the executive asks for a recommendation, show alternatives and evidence; do not present personal preference as organizational consensus.

## Facilitate disagreement toward a decision

> **In short:** Surface the interests and evidence behind opposing positions, then clarify who decides.

Start by restating the shared outcome and the disagreement without assigning blame. Give each party time to describe the risk they are protecting. Ask what evidence would change their view and what constraints are real.

In the case, operations may want local matching thresholds while finance wants global consistency. Options might include a common minimum control with region-specific tolerances, a pilot by transaction type, or a phased standardization. Compare them against control, cost, speed and maintainability.

The senior BA facilitates analysis but does not force consensus when decision rights belong to a sponsor or control owner. If the group cannot agree, present a recommendation, consequences and the accountable decision-maker. Record dissent and accepted risk accurately.

## Work with ambiguity and hypotheses

> **In short:** Make uncertainty visible, test the riskiest assumptions and update the problem statement.

Ambiguity is normal in early discovery. Maintain an assumption log with the statement, why it matters, confidence, owner and test. Rank assumptions by impact if false and how quickly they can be tested.

Use a hypothesis to focus evidence gathering: “The largest source of aged breaks is inconsistent reference data.” Compare exception data across regions, inspect representative records and interview analysts. Avoid confirmation bias by looking for evidence against the hypothesis too.

When uncertainty is high, use a timeboxed spike, prototype, sample analysis or pilot. Define what decision the experiment will inform and what result counts as support or rejection. Do not call an experiment successful merely because it was completed.

Update scope and recommendation as evidence changes. A senior BA communicates what is known, what is not, and which decisions can safely wait.

## Make decision rights explicit

> **In short:** Identify who recommends, who decides, who must be consulted and who records the outcome.

A RACI can clarify work responsibilities, but it is often too broad for consequential decisions. Create a decision register for choices such as control thresholds, data retention, architecture boundaries and rollout sequence. Include decision owner, recommender, consulted groups, deadline, options, rationale and conditions.

Separate approval authority from expertise. An engineer may advise on integration risk while a business sponsor chooses between cost and timing. A control owner may have veto authority over a control design. Confirm these rights early.

Escalate when a decision is overdue, the owner is unclear, risks exceed tolerance or teams are acting on incompatible assumptions. Bring options and impacts rather than simply forwarding a conflict.

## Facilitate across regions and cultures

> **In short:** Design participation so location, language, seniority and time zone do not hide important evidence.

For distributed groups, rotate meeting times where practical, send pre-reads early, use plain language, define acronyms and offer asynchronous input. In a live workshop, invite quieter roles before senior voices dominate and separate idea generation from evaluation.

Be careful when interpreting silence as agreement. Ask participants to confirm decisions in writing and provide a private channel for concerns. Use interpreters or translated materials where needed; do not assume fluency from job title.

Compare regional processes without labeling one “wrong.” Ask what regulation, customer need, system limitation or history explains the difference. The goal is to find a workable target, not to erase context.

After sessions, publish decisions, actions and unresolved issues with owners. Verify that participants understood the same outcome.
