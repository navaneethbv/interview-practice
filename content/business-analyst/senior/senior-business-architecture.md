Business architecture helps leaders understand how a proposed change affects the enterprise as a whole. In the reconciliation case, a target is not only a new matching application: roles, controls, data, service levels, regional processes and support may all change.

## Model capabilities and value streams

> **In short:** Use capabilities and value streams to connect organizational ability to end-to-end outcomes.

A capability map describes what the organization must be able to do, independent of current teams or systems. A value stream shows the stages through which a stakeholder receives value. Together they reveal whether the problem sits in one activity or across handoffs.

Keep the map at a level that supports decisions. For reconciliation, capabilities might include ingest financial data, match transactions, investigate exceptions, approve adjustments and evidence controls. The value stream follows an item from source receipt to resolution and reporting.

Overlay performance, pain points, systems and ownership only where evidence exists. Do not treat a colorful map as analysis by itself. Use it to identify gaps, duplication, dependencies and investment choices.

## Describe the current state with evidence

> **In short:** Combine process, people, information, systems and measures to explain how work actually happens.

Current-state analysis should include formal procedures and real practice. Compare process maps with interviews, observation, system logs, queue data and control evidence. Record where sources disagree and how confident you are.

For the case, measure volume, auto-match rate, break age, manual touches, write-offs, late feeds and close delays by region and transaction type. Verify definitions before comparing. A “break” may be counted differently by different teams.

Include constraints such as regulation, contracts, platform end-of-life, staff capacity and calendar windows. Separate fixed constraints from assumptions that can be revisited.

A senior BA presents a balanced picture: what works, where value is lost, why the evidence is credible and what remains unknown.

## Design a target operating model

> **In short:** Describe the future roles, processes, data, systems, controls and measures together.

A target operating model explains how the organization will operate after change. It should cover people and accountability, processes, information, technology, partners, locations, policies and performance management.

Define the intended outcome before drawing the model. For each changed responsibility, identify who performs the work, who decides, what information they need, what system supports them and what control applies.

In the case, standardize the global minimum for evidence and reporting while allowing justified regional variations. Define an exception governance mechanism so local flexibility does not become undocumented drift.

Validate the model with frontline users, operations, technology and control owners. A target that only fits the steering committee is not operationally ready.

## Analyze gaps and transition states

> **In short:** Compare current and target capabilities, then define the smallest safe steps between them.

A gap analysis identifies missing capabilities, changed responsibilities, data or technology needs, controls and skills. Prioritize gaps by outcome impact, risk, dependency and effort. Avoid labeling every difference as a defect.

Most transformations need intermediate states. A region may first standardize data definitions, then introduce automated matching, then retire manual controls after evidence shows the replacement works. Each transition needs entry criteria, exit criteria, owner and rollback or fallback path.

Identify temporary coexistence risks, duplicate processing, reconciliation between old and new systems, training and support. Sequence migration around close calendars and regulatory commitments.

## Create a business architecture roadmap

> **In short:** Sequence capability and operating-model changes into decision-ready increments.

A roadmap connects target capabilities to initiatives, dependencies, timing, outcomes and decision gates. It should distinguish committed delivery from options or horizons. Show enabling work such as data cleanup, identity/access, control design and support readiness.

Use scenario planning where funding, vendor selection or regulation is uncertain. State what changes under each scenario and which decisions are time-critical.

Prioritize increments that produce evidence, reduce risk or unlock later value. Do not optimize a roadmap for activity count. Review it when assumptions, dependencies or business priorities change.

## Evaluate organizational and ecosystem impacts

> **In short:** Look beyond the project boundary to affected partners, policies and customers.

Enterprise change can affect vendors, correspondent banks, auditors, customers, regulators, shared services and adjacent product teams. Map interfaces, obligations, service commitments and decision owners across the ecosystem.

Check impacts on roles, skills, capacity, policies, support hours, reporting and records retention. In a multi-region model, consider language, local holidays, time zones and regulatory variation.

Identify who must approve changes outside the team’s authority. A senior BA coordinates analysis across these boundaries and makes unresolved ownership visible.

Use this impact map in business case, governance, change and rollout planning.
