# Write a resume that gets interviews

A resume has one job: convince a recruiter, in under a minute, that you are worth a phone screen.
This guide is general advice for software engineering roles; adapt it to your market and seniority.
Reviewed October 11, 2026.

## Structure

- One page for most candidates with under about ten years of experience; two at most.
- Order: contact details and links, experience (most recent first), projects if they add evidence, education, skills.
- A clean single-column layout with standard section headings parses reliably in applicant tracking systems.

## Bullet points that carry evidence

Write each bullet as action, scope and result:

```text
Weak:   Worked on the payments service.
Better: Rebuilt the payments retry pipeline in Go, cutting failed charges from 1.8% to 0.6% across 2M daily transactions.
```

Lead with the verb, include the technology only when it matters to the reader, and quantify honestly.
If you have no number, describe a verifiable outcome: "adopted by four teams", "removed a weekly manual migration".
Every bullet is a possible interview question; do not list anything you cannot discuss in depth.

## Tailoring

Keep a longer master resume and tailor a copy for each role.
Mirror the job description's important terms where they are true for you, and move the most relevant experience to the top of each section.

## Checklist

- No typos, consistent tense and dates.
- Links to GitHub or a portfolio work and show something you are proud of.
- No photos, birth dates or other personal details unless they are normal in the target country.

# Navigate the hiring process

## Getting interviews

Referrals have the highest response rate: ask former colleagues and people you know at the company, and make it easy by sending the job link and a two-line summary of why you fit.
Recruiter outreach on professional networks works better with a complete profile that matches your resume.
For cold applications, apply early after a role is posted and tailor the resume.

## A typical loop

1. Recruiter call: background, interests, logistics and compensation expectations.
2. Technical screen: one or two coding problems, sometimes an online assessment.
3. Onsite or virtual loop: several coding rounds, system design for mid-level and above, a behavioral round, and sometimes a domain or AI-assisted round.
4. Hiring committee or debrief, team matching, and an offer.

Ask the recruiter early for the exact round types, the languages allowed, whether an AI assistant is available, and the preparation material the company publishes.

## Managing several processes

Keep a tracker with each company's stage, dates and contacts.
Try to align final rounds and offers in the same few weeks; it is normal to tell a recruiter you have other processes and ask them to move faster or slower.

# Prepare on a schedule

## Plan backwards from the interview date

| Time before the loop | Focus |
| --- | --- |
| 12 to 16 weeks | Patterns and core lists (the 16-week plan on the Practice page), system design fundamentals |
| 4 to 8 weeks | Company lists, timed problems, design case studies, story bank |
| 2 to 4 weeks | Mock interviews twice a week, review queue, behavioral rehearsal |
| Final week | Light review, rest, logistics |

## Habits that compound

- Time every problem and stop after a fixed limit; read the walkthrough and add the problem to the review queue.
- Speak while you solve, even alone.
- Do weekly mock interviews with a partner or the Practice page's mock mode, and debrief honestly.
- Track weak patterns and schedule them, rather than solving only what you already enjoy.

## The day of the interview

Test your camera, microphone, IDE and network beforehand.
Keep water, paper and a pen nearby.
Between rounds, reset rather than replaying the last one; each interviewer usually scores independently.

# Evaluate and negotiate offers

## Understand the components

- **Base salary.**
- **Bonus:** target percentage and how it has actually paid out.
- **Equity:** grant value, vesting schedule (for example four years with a one-year cliff or front-loaded schedules), and for private companies the type (options or RSUs), strike price and recent valuation.
- **Sign-on bonus** and any clawback terms.
- **Level and title**, which affect future raises and promotion timing.
- Benefits, remote policy, and relocation.

Compare offers on total annual compensation over the first few years and on the work itself.
Public compensation data sites help you understand typical ranges by company, level and location.

## Negotiating

- Wait for the written offer before negotiating the details.
- Be collaborative and specific: "I'm excited about the team. I have a competing offer with a higher equity grant; is there flexibility on equity or a sign-on bonus?"
- Base salary is often constrained by level bands; equity and sign-on bonuses usually have more room.
- Ask whether a higher level is possible if your interviews supported it; level matters more over time than a one-time bonus.
- Only mention competing offers that exist, and be ready to share them if asked.
- Get every agreed change in writing before accepting.

## Deciding

Write down what matters to you, such as the team, the manager, the growth path, stability and compensation, and score each offer.
Ask to speak with the manager or a future teammate if you still have questions.
Decline other offers promptly and politely; you may meet those recruiters again.
