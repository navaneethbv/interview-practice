A senior BA helps leaders decide whether a change is worth doing and whether it delivered what was promised. The reconciliation case needs a business case before funding and a benefits review after rollout.

## Build an evidence-based business case

> **In short:** Compare options using costs, benefits, risk, assumptions and time to value.

A business case describes the need, options, expected outcomes, costs, risks, assumptions, dependencies and recommendation. Include a credible do-nothing or minimal-change option. Separate one-time investment from recurring operating cost.

For financial evaluation, work with finance on appropriate measures such as net present value, payback period or return on investment. State the time horizon, discount rate and assumptions. Avoid false precision when volumes, savings or adoption are uncertain.

Include non-financial value such as control strength, customer experience, resilience or regulatory readiness, with evidence and a clear description. Show sensitivity to assumptions that could change the choice.

The sponsor owns the investment decision. The senior BA makes the decision useful by exposing uncertainty and trade-offs.

## Create a benefits map

> **In short:** Trace investment to capability change, stakeholder behavior and measurable business outcomes.

A benefits map links an initiative’s outputs to changed capabilities or behaviors and then to outcomes and strategic objectives. A new matching engine is an output; reduced investigation effort is a behavior or capability change; lower aged breaks may be an outcome.

For each benefit, name a business owner, measure, baseline, target, expected date, data source and dependencies. Identify disbenefits too, such as fewer manual tasks changing role design or new exception queues increasing support workload.

Check whether benefits depend on process adoption, policy decisions, data quality or another project. If so, make those prerequisites visible in the plan and governance.

## Establish baselines and metrics

> **In short:** Define the measure consistently before setting a target.

A baseline describes current performance using an agreed period, population, calculation and data source. A target states the expected future result and date. Validate whether the source data is complete and comparable across regions.

For the case, define “aged break,” “auto-match rate,” “manual touch” and “write-off error” precisely. Segment by region, value, transaction type or cause where it changes the decision. Avoid comparing unlike populations.

Use leading indicators such as data completeness, adoption or test pass rates to spot problems early, alongside lagging outcomes such as close duration or aged-break reduction. Document measure owner and reporting cadence.

## Assign benefit ownership

> **In short:** Benefits need accountable business owners after the project team moves on.

The delivery team can build an output, but the business usually changes process and realizes ongoing benefits. Assign an owner with authority to change procedures, staffing, training or policy. Agree who tracks the measure and who acts if it misses target.

Clarify handoff from project to operations, including the measurement period, data access, support and review forum. A benefits register without active owners is only a list.

If a benefit depends on another team, document the dependency and decision path. Escalate unowned or unfunded benefits before the investment is approved.

## Analyze uncertainty and sensitivity

> **In short:** Show how the recommendation changes when important assumptions move.

Estimate ranges where evidence is weak. Test high-impact assumptions such as adoption rate, avoided manual effort, transaction growth, vendor cost and implementation timing. A sensitivity table can show which variables matter most.

Use scenario analysis for materially different futures, such as a regulatory deadline moving or a region not being ready. Distinguish probability from impact and avoid treating all assumptions as equally uncertain.

Recommend a pilot or staged investment when it buys useful evidence at acceptable cost. State what result would justify continuing, changing direction or stopping.

## Review realized value

> **In short:** Compare actual outcomes with the baseline, explain variance and decide what to do next.

A post-implementation review asks whether the change was adopted, whether expected outcomes occurred, what unintended effects appeared and what actions remain. Choose review timing that allows benefits to emerge, and use the same definitions as the baseline.

Separate delivery success from benefit success. A release can meet scope and schedule while failing to reduce aged breaks because upstream data remains poor. Explain variance with evidence rather than assigning blame.

Recommend corrective actions, further investment, process changes or closure. Capture lessons for future business cases and update forecasts so leaders learn from experience.
