BAs succeed through other people. Interviewers spend a lot of time on communication, conflict and influence — usually through "tell me about a time…" questions. This chapter gives you practical techniques and the words to describe them.

## Writing for busy people

> **In short:** Bottom line first, short sentences, one idea per paragraph.

### BLUF — bottom line up front

Start with the conclusion or the request, then the reasons. Readers decide in the first two lines whether to keep reading.

> *Weak:* "Following our discussions over the last few weeks with several teams, and after reviewing the data, there are some concerns…"
> *Strong:* "**Decision needed by Friday:** approve a two-week delay to UAT so we can fix three critical defects. Details below."

### Email and document tips

- A clear **subject line** with the action: "Approval needed: UAT start date change".
- **Short paragraphs** and bullet points for lists.
- **Plain words** — "use" not "utilize", "start" not "commence".
- **Active voice** — "The team fixed the defect" not "The defect was fixed".
- **Define acronyms** the first time.
- Say exactly **who** needs to do **what** by **when**.
- **Proofread** names, numbers and dates — errors damage trust.

### Meeting minutes

Record **decisions, actions (owner + date) and open issues** — not a transcript. Send within 24 hours and ask for corrections.

## Speaking to different audiences

> **In short:** Same facts, different level of detail.

| Audience | What they want | How to present |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Executives** | The "so what": impact, risk, cost, decision | One page or 3 slides; conclusion first; numbers |
| **Business users** | What changes for *them* and when | Demos, examples, before/after process |
| **Developers** | Precise behavior, rules, data, edge cases | Detailed stories, acceptance criteria, models |
| **Testers** | How to prove it works | Scenarios, expected results, test data |
| **Auditors / compliance** | Controls, evidence, traceability | Formal documents, sign-offs, audit trails |

### Translating between business and technology

A BA's special skill is translation. Avoid jargon with business users ("the API returns a 500 error" → "the system can't fetch the data right now"). With developers, replace vague business language with precise rules ("process urgent items first" → "sort items with priority = HIGH before others, then by oldest value date").

### Presenting tips

- Know your **one key message** before you start.
- Use **visuals** — a process diagram beats a paragraph.
- **Tell a story**: problem → insight → recommendation.
- Leave time for questions; if you don't know an answer, say "I'll check and come back to you by tomorrow" — then do it.

## Handling conflict

> **In short:** Focus on interests, not positions, and keep returning to the shared goal.

Conflict between stakeholders is normal; BAs often sit in the middle.

### Positions versus interests

- A **position** is what someone says they want: "We need a manual approval step."
- An **interest** is *why* they want it: "I'm worried about an error reaching the general ledger."

When you find the interest, there may be another solution that satisfies everyone — for example an automated validation plus approval only for high-value items.

### A conflict-resolution routine

1. **Listen to each side separately** and let them feel heard.
2. **Find the interests** behind the positions.
3. **Reframe around the shared goal** and the agreed business objectives.
4. **Use facts and data** — volumes, costs, risks — to depersonalize the discussion.
5. **Generate options** together.
6. **Agree** — or, if needed, **escalate** with options and their trade-offs to the decision maker.
7. **Document** the decision and the reason.

### The Thomas-Kilmann conflict modes

| Mode | When it fits |
| --- | --- |
| **Collaborating** (win-win) | Important issue, time to find a solution that meets both sides' interests |
| **Compromising** (split the difference) | Moderate importance, need a quick workable answer |
| **Competing** (assert your view) | Urgent, or a matter of compliance or ethics |
| **Accommodating** (give way) | The issue matters more to the other person; to build goodwill |
| **Avoiding** (postpone) | Trivial issue, or emotions need time to cool |

Collaborating is usually best for a BA, but good professionals choose the mode that fits the situation.

## Negotiating and saying no

> **In short:** Say "yes, and here is the trade-off" instead of a flat "no".

BAs negotiate scope, priorities and timelines constantly.

### Useful approaches

- **Trade-offs, not refusals** — "We can add that feature this release if we move the reporting change to the next one. Which matters more?"
- **Refer to agreed priorities** — "Our sprint goal is X. Does this new item support it more than what we've planned?"
- **Make the cost visible** — "Adding this means two more weeks of testing and moves go-live past month-end."
- **Know your BATNA** — your *Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement*: what you'll do if there's no agreement. It tells you how flexible you can be.
- **Separate the person from the problem** (from *Getting to Yes* by Fisher and Ury): be soft on people, firm on principles.

### Pushing back on a senior stakeholder

Be respectful, specific and evidence-based: acknowledge their goal, explain the impact or risk with data, propose alternatives, and let the accountable person decide. Disagreeing professionally is a strength interviewers look for.

## Influencing without authority

> **In short:** BAs rarely manage the people they need — so they earn trust.

Ways to influence when you're not the boss:

- **Credibility** — be accurate, prepared and reliable. Do what you said you'd do.
- **Relationships** — invest in people before you need them.
- **What's in it for them?** — frame proposals around the other person's goals.
- **Data and evidence** — facts persuade better than opinions.
- **Involve people early** — people support what they help create.
- **Find allies and champions** — respected people who support the change.
- **Small wins** — a successful pilot builds momentum for the bigger change.

Robert Cialdini's principles of persuasion are also useful to know: reciprocity, commitment and consistency, social proof, authority, liking and scarcity (he later added unity).

## Working across cultures and languages

> **In short:** Be clear, check understanding, and respect different styles.

Global teams span countries, time zones and languages. Practical habits:

- **Plain English** — short sentences, no idioms ("ballpark", "touch base"), no slang.
- **Confirm in writing** after calls, especially when people are working in a second language.
- **Visuals** reduce dependence on language.
- **Check understanding** with open questions ("How would you explain the next step to your team?") rather than "Do you understand?" — many cultures avoid saying no.
- **Respect communication styles** — some cultures are direct, others indirect; some expect hierarchy, others are flat.
- **Rotate meeting times** so the same region isn't always on late calls.
- **Know local differences** — holidays, date formats (03/04 is 3 April in Australia but March 4 in the US), working weeks and regulations.

Speaking several languages is an asset: it builds rapport and helps you understand how meaning can get lost in translation. Mention it in interviews when the role involves international stakeholders.

## Training users and transferring knowledge

> **In short:** Teach in small steps, practice, check understanding, give feedback.

BAs often train users on a new process or system and onboard new team members. Good teaching techniques transfer directly:

- **Know the learner** — their current knowledge, goals and pace; adapt the material.
- **Small chunks** — one concept at a time, then practice.
- **Show, then do** — demonstrate, then have the learner try with real examples.
- **Check understanding** — ask learners to explain or demonstrate, not just "any questions?".
- **Constructive feedback** — specific, timely and balanced: what went well, what to improve, how.
- **Job aids** — quick reference guides, short videos, FAQs and SOPs for after the training.
- **Measure** — error rates and questions after training show whether it worked.

### Adult learning (andragogy)

Adults learn best when the learning is **relevant to their job**, **practical**, builds on **their experience**, and lets them **solve real problems**.

### Train-the-trainer

For large rollouts, train **super users** or **change champions** in each team, who then train their colleagues and become the first point of contact.

## Ethics, confidentiality and impartiality

> **In short:** Be fair, honest and discreet.

BAs see sensitive information — salaries, customer data, restructuring plans, audit findings. Trust is essential.

- **Confidentiality** — share information only with people who need it; follow data-protection rules; never discuss sensitive details casually.
- **Impartiality** — represent all stakeholders' needs fairly, weigh evidence, and don't favor the loudest or most senior voice.
- **Honesty** — report status truthfully, including bad news. Don't inflate numbers.
- **Declare conflicts of interest.**
- **Respect** — treat every stakeholder with courtesy, regardless of seniority.

IIBA members and certification holders agree to follow the **IIBA Code of Ethical Conduct**, which expects **integrity**, **competence** and **professional responsibility** — including protecting confidential information.

> **Interview tip:** If asked "Tell me about an ethical dilemma," choose a story where you were honest about a problem (a missed deadline, an error, a risk) even when it was uncomfortable, and explain the outcome.
