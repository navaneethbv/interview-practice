## How BA interviews work

> **In short:** Expect three to five conversations, each testing something different.

A typical process:

1. **Recruiter screen (20–30 min)** — background, motivation, salary expectations, work authorization, availability.
2. **Hiring manager (45–60 min)** — your experience, how you work, behavioral questions, fit with the team.
3. **Technical or skills round** — requirements writing, process modeling, SQL/Excel, domain knowledge, sometimes a live exercise ("write user stories for this feature").
4. **Panel or case interview** — several stakeholders (PM, developer, business lead) or a case study you present.
5. **Final / leadership round** — values, motivation, big-picture thinking.

### What interviewers are checking

- Can you **understand a business problem** and ask good questions?
- Can you **communicate clearly** to both business and technical people?
- Do you know the **standard techniques** and when to use them?
- Have you **delivered results**, and can you prove it?
- Will you be **easy and trustworthy to work with**?

### Prepare for each company

Read the job description line by line and match each requirement to a story or skill. Research the company's products, recent news, and the industry's hot topics (for banks: ISO 20022, real-time payments, regulatory change, automation and AI).

## "Tell me about yourself"

> **In short:** Ninety seconds — present, past, future.

This is almost always the first question. Use **present → past → future**:

1. **Present** — who you are professionally now, in one or two sentences. *"I'm a business analyst with experience in banking operations, requirements and process improvement."*
2. **Past** — two or three highlights that prove you can do this job, each with a result. *"Most recently I… which reduced…"*
3. **Future** — why this role and this company. *"I'm excited about this role because…"*

### Tips

- Keep it to **60–90 seconds**. Practise out loud with a timer.
- Choose highlights that match the **job description**.
- Use one or two **numbers**.
- End with the **future** — it hands the conversation back naturally.
- Don't recite your resume line by line or start from school.

### Explaining moves, gaps and changes

If you have moved countries, changed industries or had a gap, address it briefly and positively, then move forward: *"After relocating, I took time to settle and prepare for the US market, including completing training in…, and I'm now focused on BA roles in financial services."* One sentence is enough — interviewers care more about what you can do next.

### "Why business analysis?"

Connect what you enjoy with what BAs do: solving problems, understanding people's needs, making processes work better, and seeing measurable results.

## The STAR method

> **In short:** Situation, Task, Action, Result — and most of the time goes on Action.

Behavioral questions ("Tell me about a time when…") are best answered with **STAR**:

| Part | What to say | Share of the answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Situation** | Context: where, when, what was happening | ~10% |
| **Task** | Your responsibility or the goal | ~10% |
| **Action** | What **you** did, step by step, and why | ~60% |
| **Result** | The outcome, with numbers if possible, plus what you learned | ~20% |

### Common STAR mistakes

- **"We" instead of "I"** — interviewers want *your* contribution. Say "I organized…", "I analyzed…".
- **Too much situation** — get to the action quickly.
- **No result** — always finish with an outcome and, ideally, a lesson.
- **Vague results** — "it went well" vs "SLA compliance rose to 96% and stayed there for six months".
- **Invented or inflated numbers** — interviewers probe. If you don't have an exact figure, give an honest approximation and say how you know.

### Example (generic)

*"Tell me about a time you improved a process."*

- **S:** Our team handled reconciliation exceptions by email, and items often waited days for an answer.
- **T:** I was asked to reduce turnaround without adding staff.
- **A:** I mapped the as-is process with the analysts and found that most delay was waiting for approvals on low-value items. I analyzed three months of data to show the value distribution, proposed an approval threshold, wrote the new procedure and requirements for a simple workflow, got sign-off from the control owner, and trained the team.
- **R:** Average turnaround fell by about a third within two months, with no increase in errors. I learned that the biggest delays are often in the waiting, not the work.

## Build your story bank

> **In short:** Prepare eight stories that can answer fifty questions.

Prepare **one or two stories for each theme** below, written in STAR form. Many stories cover several themes.

| Theme | Prompt to find your story |
| --- | --- |
| **Requirements** | A time your requirements prevented defects or rework |
| **Elicitation** | A difficult interview or workshop you ran |
| **Stakeholder conflict** | Two people or teams wanted different things |
| **Influence** | You convinced someone without having authority |
| **Process improvement** | You made a process faster, cheaper or more accurate |
| **Root cause / problem solving** | You found why a recurring problem kept happening |
| **Data / reporting** | You used data or a dashboard to change a decision |
| **Delivery under pressure** | Tight deadline, several projects at once |
| **Testing / production issue** | A defect or incident you triaged and resolved |
| **Mistake or failure** | Something went wrong and what you learned |
| **Ambiguity** | You had to start with unclear requirements |
| **Teaching / coaching** | You helped someone learn or adopt a change |
| **Attention to detail** | Your accuracy caught an important error |
| **Ethics / confidentiality** | You handled sensitive information or a difficult truth |

### For each story, note

- The **numbers** (before and after) and where they came from.
- **Your** specific actions.
- The **technique names** from this course (5 Whys, BPMN, RACI, MoSCoW, UAT…) — using the right vocabulary signals expertise.
- A **lesson learned**.

## Common BA interview questions

> **In short:** Know the framework for each answer; fill it with your stories.

### Role and process

| Question | Framework for your answer |
| --- | --- |
| What does a BA do? | Define needs, recommend solutions, deliver value; bridge business and technology |
| How do you gather requirements? | Stakeholder analysis → choose techniques (interviews, workshops, observation, documents) → document → confirm → validate → manage change |
| How do you handle changing requirements? | Baseline, impact analysis via traceability, options, decision by owner, update documents |
| How do you prioritize? | MoSCoW or value vs effort, tied to business objectives, agreed with the PO/sponsor |
| BRD vs FRD? | Business needs (what/why) vs system functions (what the system does) |
| Functional vs non-functional? | Behavior vs quality attributes, with examples |
| What makes a good user story? | INVEST + clear acceptance criteria |
| How do you make sure requirements are understood? | Walkthroughs, examples, acceptance criteria, prototypes, review with QA |
| Agile vs waterfall — which do you prefer? | Both have a place; choose by uncertainty and constraints; give experience in each |
| How do you know a project succeeded? | Measured against the business objectives and KPIs defined at the start |

### Behavioral

- Tell me about a difficult stakeholder.
- Describe a time you disagreed with your manager.
- Tell me about a project that failed or a mistake you made.
- How do you manage several projects at once?
- Tell me about a time you had to learn something quickly.
- Describe a time you used data to influence a decision.
- Tell me about a time you found a problem others had missed.

### Domain (banking)

- Walk me through a reconciliation process.
- What causes reconciliation breaks, and how would you reduce them?
- What is a nostro account?
- How do debits and credits work?
- What controls would you expect around a payments process?
- What is ISO 20022, and why does it matter?

### Technical

- Write a SQL query to find accounts with more than five unmatched items. *(GROUP BY + HAVING)*
- How would you find records in table A that aren't in table B? *(LEFT JOIN … IS NULL)*
- Which Excel functions do you use most? *(XLOOKUP/VLOOKUP, SUMIFS, pivot tables)*
- Explain the difference between an exclusive and a parallel gateway in BPMN.

## Case studies and live exercises

> **In short:** Think out loud, ask clarifying questions, structure before detail.

Some interviews include a case: *"Our customer complaints about delayed payments have doubled. How would you approach this?"*

### A structure that works for almost any case

1. **Clarify the goal and scope** — "What does 'delayed' mean here? Which customers and payment types? What's the target?"
2. **Identify stakeholders** — customers, operations, IT, compliance, the payment network.
3. **Understand the current state** — map the payment process; get data on volumes, delays, where delays happen.
4. **Find root causes** — Pareto of delay reasons, 5 Whys on the top ones.
5. **Generate options** — quick wins and longer-term fixes (process, system, policy, training).
6. **Evaluate** — cost, benefit, risk, feasibility; recommend one.
7. **Define success** — KPIs and how you'll measure them.
8. **Plan delivery** — requirements, testing, rollout, change management.

### Live writing exercises

- **Write user stories** for a feature (for example "customers can dispute a card transaction"). Include the persona, the benefit, and 3–5 acceptance criteria with happy, edge and error cases.
- **Draw a process** on a whiteboard — start/end events, swimlanes, decisions, exceptions.
- **Critique a requirement** — spot ambiguity, missing NFRs, non-atomic statements.

### Tips

- **Ask questions before solving** — it's what BAs do, and interviewers notice.
- **Think out loud** so they can follow your reasoning.
- **State assumptions** explicitly.
- It's fine to say, "Let me take a moment to structure my thoughts."

## Questions to ask the interviewer

> **In short:** Good questions show you're thinking about doing the job well.

Prepare five; ask two or three.

- What would success look like in this role after six months?
- What are the biggest challenges facing the team right now?
- How do BAs work with product owners, developers and testers here?
- Which delivery approach does the team use — Scrum, Kanban, hybrid?
- What tools does the team use for requirements and modeling?
- How are requirements reviewed and signed off?
- What does a typical week look like for a BA on this team?
- How do you support professional development and certifications?
- What are the next steps in the process?

Avoid asking about salary, vacation or remote work in the first interview unless the interviewer raises it — save those for the recruiter or the offer stage.

## Your resume, LinkedIn and the job search

> **In short:** Tailor every application, use the job's keywords, and show results.

### Resume tips for BA roles

- A **headline** that matches the target role: "Business Analyst | Banking Operations | Requirements & Process Improvement".
- A short **summary** (3–4 lines) aimed at BA work.
- **Bullet points that start with action verbs** and end with results: *"Facilitated workshops with 12 stakeholders to define to-be process, reducing turnaround by X%."*
- **Keywords** from the job description (BRD, user stories, UAT, Jira, SQL, BPMN, Agile, stakeholder management) — many companies use **applicant tracking systems (ATS)** that scan for them.
- **One to two pages**, simple formatting (ATS systems struggle with tables, columns and images).
- **Tailor** the resume to each role — a reconciliation-focused BA role and a product-focused BA role need different emphasis.

### LinkedIn

- Headline and "About" aligned with your target role.
- Add skills (Business Analysis, Requirements Gathering, UAT, SQL, Jira, Process Mapping).
- Set "Open to work" visible to recruiters.
- Connect with BA professionals and recruiters in financial services; join your local **IIBA chapter** (many run free or low-cost events that also count toward professional development hours).

### Where to look

LinkedIn Jobs, Indeed, company career sites (banks, payment companies, insurers, consultancies), staffing agencies that specialize in IT and finance, and referrals — referrals are often the fastest route to an interview.

## Interview day and follow-up

> **In short:** Prepare the logistics, stay calm, follow up within a day.

### Before

- Re-read the job description and your story bank.
- Prepare your "tell me about yourself" and three key stories that best match the role.
- For video interviews: test camera, microphone and connection; good light in front of you; quiet room; close notifications.
- Have water, a notepad, your resume and your questions ready.

### During

- Smile, listen to the full question, and pause before answering.
- If a question is unclear, ask: "Do you mean…?"
- Keep answers to **about two minutes**; offer more detail if they want it.
- Take brief notes — it shows you're engaged.

### After

- Send a short **thank-you email within 24 hours**: thank them, mention one specific thing you discussed, and restate your interest.
- Write down the questions you were asked and how you answered — it improves the next interview.
- If you don't get the job, ask politely for feedback. Every interview is practice.
